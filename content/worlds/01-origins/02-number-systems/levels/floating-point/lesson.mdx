<Step id="hook" section="hook" title="From atoms to galaxies in 32 bits">

The mass of an electron is about $9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg; the mass of the Sun about $2 \times 10^{30}$ kg. Fixed point cannot hold both in any reasonable width. Scientists solved this centuries ago with **scientific notation** — and IEEE 754 floating point is scientific notation in binary.

</Step>

<Step id="intuition-sci" section="intuition" title="Significant digits plus an exponent">

$6.02 \times 10^{23}$ stores a few **significant digits** (6.02) and a **power** (23). The point "floats" to wherever the exponent puts it. You keep the same number of significant digits whether the number is tiny or huge: precision is **relative**, not absolute.

In binary: $-6.25 = -110.01_2 = -1.1001_2 \times 2^{2}$.

</Step>

<Step id="formal-layout" section="formal" title="The binary32 and binary64 layouts">

| Format | Sign | Exponent | Fraction | Bias | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| binary32 ("float") | 1 bit | 8 bits | 23 bits | 127 | 24 significant bits ≈ 7 decimal digits |
| binary64 ("double") | 1 bit | 11 bits | 52 bits | 1023 | 53 bits ≈ 15–16 decimal digits |

For a **normal** number, with stored exponent field $e$ and fraction field $f$:

$$
v = (-1)^{s} \times 1.f_2 \times 2^{\,e - \text{bias}}
$$

The leading 1 is **not stored** (the "hidden bit"), which buys one extra bit of precision.

</Step>

<Step id="formal-special" section="formal" title="Special values">

| Exponent field | Fraction | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| all 0 | 0 | ±0 (two zeros exist) |
| all 0 | ≠ 0 | **Subnormal**: $(-1)^s \times 0.f \times 2^{1-\text{bias}}$ — gradual underflow toward 0 |
| 1 … 254 (binary32) | any | Normal number |
| all 1 | 0 | ±∞ (e.g. 1/0) |
| all 1 | ≠ 0 | **NaN** — "not a number" (e.g. 0/0, √−1) |

Largest binary32 ≈ $3.4 \times 10^{38}$; smallest normal ≈ $1.18 \times 10^{-38}$; smallest subnormal $2^{-149} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-45}$.

</Step>

<Step id="worked-example" section="worked-example" title="Encoding −6.25 by hand">

1. Binary: $6.25 = 110.01_2$.
2. Normalise: $1.1001_2 \times 2^2$.
3. Sign $s = 1$ (negative).
4. Exponent field $e = 2 + 127 = 129 = 1000\,0001_2$.
5. Fraction: the bits after the leading 1 → `1001` followed by 19 zeros.

Bits: `1 | 10000001 | 10010000000000000000000` = **0xC0C80000**.

Decoding goes in reverse: $(-1)^1 \times 1.1001_2 \times 2^{129-127} = -1.5625 \times 4 = -6.25$ ✓.

</Step>

<Step id="tradeoffs" section="tradeoffs" title="What floating point trades">

| Gains | Costs |
|---|---|
| Enormous range in few bits | Most decimal fractions (0.1, 0.2 …) are only approximated |
| Constant *relative* precision | Gaps between neighbouring values grow with magnitude: above $2^{24}$ a float cannot represent every integer |
| Standard behaviour on every platform (IEEE 754) | Rounding makes algebra laws fail — see the next level |

</Step>

<Step id="pitfalls" section="pitfalls" title="Common misconceptions">

<Callout kind="warning">**"The exponent field is the exponent."** It is stored with a **bias**: field 127 means $2^0$ in binary32.</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">**"A float holds 32 bits of precision."** Only 24 significant bits (23 stored + hidden 1); the other bits are sign and exponent.</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">**"Floats can store every integer up to $3.4 \times 10^{38}$."** Every integer is exact only up to $2^{24} = 16{,}777{,}216$; $16{,}777{,}217$ rounds to $16{,}777{,}216$. For doubles the limit is $2^{53}$.</Callout>

</Step>

<Step id="context" section="context" title="A standard that ended chaos">

Before 1985 every manufacturer had its own floating-point format, and the same program gave different answers on different machines. William Kahan led the work on **IEEE 754-1985**, revised in 2008 and 2019; Kahan received the 1989 Turing Award for it. GPUs and machine learning also use smaller formats: binary16 ("half") and bfloat16.

</Step>
