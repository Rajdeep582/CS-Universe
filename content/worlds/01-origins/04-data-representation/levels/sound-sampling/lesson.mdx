<Step id="hook" section="hook" title="Turning air into numbers">

Sound is air pressure changing smoothly over time. A computer can only store a list of numbers. To record sound it measures the pressure at regular instants — and if it measures often enough, the original wave can be rebuilt *exactly*.

</Step>

<Step id="intuition-two-steps" section="intuition" title="Two decisions: how often, how precisely">

Digitising a signal takes two steps:

1. **Sampling** — measure the signal at regular time intervals (the **sampling rate**, in samples per second, Hz).
2. **Quantisation** — round each measurement to one of a fixed set of levels (the **bit depth**).

Too few samples per second loses high frequencies; too few bits per sample adds noise.

</Step>

<Step id="formal-nyquist" section="formal" title="The sampling theorem">

The **Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem**: a signal containing no frequencies at or above $f_{max}$ can be reconstructed exactly from samples taken at a rate

$$
f_s > 2 f_{max}.
$$

$f_s / 2$ is the **Nyquist frequency**. Human hearing reaches about 20 kHz, so CD audio samples at **44.1 kHz**, leaving room for the anti-aliasing filter.

</Step>

<Step id="formal-quantisation" section="formal" title="Quantisation and noise">

With $N$ bits there are $2^N$ levels. Rounding to the nearest level adds **quantisation error** of at most half a step. For a full-scale sine wave the signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio is about

$$
\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02\,N + 1.76 \text{ dB}
$$

so 16-bit audio has about 98 dB of dynamic range; each extra bit buys about 6 dB.

</Step>

<Step id="mechanism-trace" section="mechanism" title="Sampling a wave by hand">

Sample $x(t) = \sin(2\pi \cdot 1000\, t)$ (a 1 kHz tone) at 8 kHz — eight samples per cycle — and quantise to 3 bits (levels −4 … 3, scaled by 4):

| Sample $n$ | $t$ (ms) | $\sin$ value | × 4 → nearest level |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 0.125 | 0.707 | 3 (2.83) |
| 2 | 0.25 | 1.000 | 3 (4 is out of range — clipped) |
| 3 | 0.375 | 0.707 | 3 |
| 4 | 0.5 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 | 0.625 | −0.707 | −3 |
| 6 | 0.75 | −1.000 | −4 |
| 7 | 0.875 | −0.707 | −3 |

The stored list is `0 3 3 3 0 −3 −4 −3`. With only 3 bits the shape is crude; with 16 bits the steps are 8,192 times finer.

</Step>

<Step id="worked-example" section="worked-example" title="How much data is a song?">

CD audio: 44,100 samples/s × 16 bits × 2 channels $= 1{,}411{,}200$ bits/s ≈ **1,411 kbit/s**, or about 10.1 MiB per minute uncompressed.

A 4-minute song: $1{,}411{,}200 \times 240 / 8 = 42{,}336{,}000$ bytes ≈ 40 MiB. A 256 kbit/s MP3 or AAC of the same song is about 7.3 MiB — over five times smaller, by discarding what listeners are unlikely to hear.

</Step>

<Step id="tradeoffs" section="tradeoffs" title="Rates and depths in practice">

| Use | Rate | Depth |
|---|---|---|
| Telephone | 8 kHz | 8-bit (companded) |
| CD | 44.1 kHz | 16-bit |
| Video soundtracks | 48 kHz | 16–24-bit |
| Studio recording | 48–96 kHz | 24-bit (headroom for editing) |

Higher numbers cost storage and processing; beyond what hearing can detect they add nothing for playback.

</Step>

<Step id="pitfalls" section="pitfalls" title="Common misconceptions">

<Callout kind="warning">**"Samples are joined by straight lines or steps."** Reconstruction uses a low-pass filter that recreates the smooth original — the "staircase" picture is only a teaching aid.</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">**Aliasing.** Frequencies above $f_s/2$ do not disappear; they fold back as false lower tones. A 5 kHz tone sampled at 8 kHz sounds like 3 kHz. That is why a filter removes them *before* sampling.</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">**"Exactly 2× is enough."** The theorem needs *more than* twice the highest frequency.</Callout>

</Step>

<Step id="context" section="context" title="Same ideas everywhere">

Sampling and quantisation also describe image sensors (spatial sampling), video frame rates (temporal sampling) and every sensor feeding a microcontroller. Harry Nyquist (1928) and Claude Shannon (1949) laid the theory down.

</Step>
