<Step id="hook" section="hook" title="Seeing simplifications">

Algebraic simplification needs insight. In 1953 Maurice Karnaugh found a way to make the insight **visible**: arrange the truth table so that terms which can be combined sit next to each other, then circle them.

</Step>

<Step id="intuition-adjacent" section="intuition" title="Neighbours differ in one variable">

The combining law $AB + AB' = A$ says: two minterms that differ in exactly one variable merge, and that variable disappears. A Karnaugh map (K-map) places the minterms on a grid where **every horizontal or vertical neighbour differs in exactly one variable**. Mergeable terms become touching cells.

</Step>

<Step id="formal-layout" section="formal" title="The layout and Gray code">

Rows and columns are labelled in **Gray code** order — 00, 01, 11, 10 — in which consecutive labels differ in one bit. The map also **wraps around**: the left and right edges are neighbours, and so are the top and bottom.

For four variables, rows are $AB$ and columns $CD$; cell (row 11, column 10) is $ABCD' = m_{14}$.

</Step>

<Step id="formal-rules" section="formal" title="Grouping rules">

1. Circle groups of 1s that are rectangles of **1, 2, 4, 8 or 16** cells (powers of two), wrapping across edges if needed.
2. Make each group **as large as possible** (larger groups → fewer literals).
3. Use **as few groups as possible** to cover every 1; groups may overlap.
4. Cells marked **X (don't care)** may be included if they help, never needed.

A group of $2^k$ cells eliminates $k$ variables: read off only the variables that stay constant across the group.

</Step>

<Step id="worked-example" section="worked-example" title="A four-variable example">

$F = \sum m(1, 3, 7, 11, 15)$ with don't-cares $d(0, 2, 5)$:

- Column $CD = 11$ holds $m_3, m_7, m_{15}, m_{11}$ — a full column of four → $CD$.
- $m_1$ remains. Group it with $m_0, m_2, m_3$ (two of them don't-cares) as the top row → $A'B'$.

$$
F = CD + A'B'
$$

(Grouping $m_1, m_3, m_5, m_7$ instead gives $CD + A'D$, equally small — minimal answers need not be unique.) Without the don't-cares, $m_1$ could only pair with $m_3$, giving the larger $A'B'D$.

</Step>

<Step id="tradeoffs" section="tradeoffs" title="Where K-maps stop working">

| K-maps | Quine–McCluskey and heuristics |
|---|---|
| Fast and visual for 2–4 variables, possible for 5–6 | Tabular, works for any number of variables, easy to program |
| Error-prone by hand for larger maps | Exact Q–M is exponential in the worst case; tools use heuristics such as Espresso |
| Produce minimal two-level SOP/POS only | Multi-level optimisation needs other methods |

The simulation itself uses Quine–McCluskey behind the map.

</Step>

<Step id="pitfalls" section="pitfalls" title="Common misconceptions">

<Callout kind="warning">**Binary order on the axes.** 00, 01, 10, 11 breaks adjacency. Use Gray code: 00, 01, 11, 10.</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">**Groups of three or six.** Groups must have a power-of-two number of cells and be rectangular.</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">**Forgetting the wrap-around.** The four corners of a 4-variable map are mutually adjacent: $\sum m(0, 2, 8, 10) = B'D'$.</Callout>

</Step>

<Step id="context" section="context" title="History">

Edward Veitch (1952) and Maurice Karnaugh at Bell Labs (1953) introduced the diagram. Quine (1952) and McCluskey (1956) gave the tabular method that computers use.

</Step>
